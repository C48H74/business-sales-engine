# Offer and value levers

Fill the [offer/pricing canvas](../templates/offer-pricing-canvas.md). Original adaptation informed by Hormozi and Stark [S15/S16](sources.md); no promised results or mandatory multipliers.

For [segment] facing [verified trigger/problem], deliver [specific artifact/service] within [feasible window], enabling [useful outcome], subject to [dependencies], excluding [limits]. Separate the deliverable you control from a downstream business outcome influenced by others.

Evaluate four levers: desired outcome, confidence in delivery, time to useful value and required customer effort. Ask which matters for this buyer. Faster delivery may cost more or increase errors; a premium buyer may value assurance more than speed. Confidence should come from relevant evidence and a feasible plan, not exaggerated certainty.

Compare buying, doing nothing, internal build and alternatives. Describe the mechanism and boundaries. Test comprehension: can an appropriate buyer explain when they would use this and what they would receive? Research a past decision before asking hypothetical willingness to pay.

Choose one offer or meaningful packages. A higher-priced package can serve a different need, but does not require an entirely different customer. Each tier needs scope, exclusions, timing, support, cost, capacity and acceptance. Avoid padding features to manufacture an anchor.

Premium design exercises are exploratory: What additional decision support is valuable? What would make the experience referable? What can be reliably repeated? Recompute economics before retaining an idea. A 10×/100× thought experiment is optional, not a pricing rule.

Validate with observable comprehension, commitments and delivery results. Record version/date, evidence and the next [experiment](../templates/experiment-card.md). Preserve an offer that works rather than redesigning it to satisfy a framework.
